We're biased. We make NFC products for a living.

So when we set out to write this, we wanted to talk to agents who'd actually shipped both---not just NFC enthusiasts. Six Melbourne agents agreed to talk on the record. Five had NFC tags running at open homes. Four still had QR signage somewhere in their workflow. Three had abandoned one of the two formats entirely.

Here's what they told us. Names stay private; agencies are real.

## The 30-second version

- **NFC wins for repeat foot traffic.** Open homes, agent cards, anything where the same physical object stays with the buyer for weeks.
- **QR wins for posters and one-off signage.** You don't need a printer at the property---just paper and a marker.
- **The deciding factor is almost always who's holding the printed thing in 10 seconds.** Stationary signage in a window? QR. Object in someone's hand? NFC.
- **Nobody who tried both went back to QR-only.** All six agents now run NFC in at least one part of their workflow.

## "QR feels like 2020. NFC feels like the property is run by professionals."

That was a quote from a Belle Property agent in South Yarra. We're not paraphrasing.

The interesting thing wasn't *that* she preferred NFC---we expected that. It was *why*. She said the QR codes on her signage worked fine, technically. Buyers scanned them, the page loaded, leads came through. The conversion wasn't bad.

But every time she handed a buyer a printed business card with a QR code on it, the buyer would stand there, take out their phone, line up the camera, and the moment took on this particular flavour. The buyer felt like they were doing the agent a favour. The agent felt like she was hawking something.

NFC inverts that. The buyer holds an *object*---a weighty 3D-printed keychain with the agency logo. They hover their phone, the page loads, and the moment doesn't feel like marketing. It feels like the agency invested in something.

We're not saying you can't run a million-dollar agency with QR codes. We're saying the *energy of the interaction* is meaningfully different.

## Where QR is still right

A Ray White agent in Carlton was emphatic: window signage stays QR.

Her reasoning was unimpeachable. The buyer is on the footpath. They're not coming inside the property tonight, possibly ever. The QR is two metres from their face on a printed A2 board. The chip in an NFC tag has to be tapped at 1-2cm range---which means the buyer would need to walk up to the window and place their phone against the glass. Awkward, not happening, doesn't scale.

QR wins on:

- **Window decals.** Distance + paper signage = perfect fit.
- **Print ads in property guides.** Same reason---reader is not going to touch the page.
- **Outdoor billboards.** Even worse for NFC. QR can be read from 5+ metres with a phone camera.

NFC wins on:

- **Handouts at the open home.** The buyer leaves with the keychain. Re-taps over the next two weeks.
- **Agent cards.** The buyer has a *thing* on their desk. Re-taps when they remember they were interested.
- **Property-specific tags.** One tag per property, with the floor plan and contact form encoded. Reorderable when the listing turns over.
- **Stair-rail / fence-rail tags.** The buyer is *inside the property*. Phone is out of pocket. Tap is more natural than camera-line-up.

## The unsolved problem: iOS background scanning

One agent we spoke to (Bayside) had abandoned NFC for six months in 2023.

Her gripe: she'd handed out keychains at three open homes, watched buyers try to tap them, and watched several phones do *nothing*. Turns out, until iOS 14 (and meaningfully smooth on iOS 16+), iPhone NFC reading required the user to be in the camera/control-centre or to have the screen unlocked in a specific state. Some older iPhones just couldn't tap-to-read at all.

That's mostly solved now. iPhone 11 and newer (which is most iPhones in active use in Australia today) do it natively. Android has been doing it since 2016.

But you do still have to write your tag payload correctly. We see a lot of cheap NFC tags from AliExpress that store the URL in a format iOS doesn't honour. We had one customer who'd bought 200 tags from offshore, programmed them with a free Android app, and couldn't figure out why none of the iPhones tapped. The chip and the encoding both have to be right.

Both of those are settable variables, not platform limitations.

## What we'd actually deploy

If you're an agent in Melbourne reading this, here's what we'd do in your first 90 days:

**Phase 1 (week 1-2).** Hand out 50 NFC keychains at your next two open homes. Track the tap analytics. See how many people who tapped also booked an inspection. This is your baseline.

**Phase 2 (week 3-6).** Add an agent NFC card to your replacement cards. Same payload as the keychain---your vCard, your portfolio, your inspection booking form. Watch the tap count from the keychain *vs* the card over a month.

**Phase 3 (week 7-12).** Add property-specific tags. One per active listing. Encoded with the property micro-site. Mounted somewhere inside the property that doesn't damage the wall.

By the end of phase 3 you'll have enough data to make an honest call. Most agents we've onboarded keep all three running.

## A note on data

Every agent we spoke to said the same thing about tap analytics: *the data is more useful than the conversions*.

The conversion rate matters, obviously. But the *temporal pattern* of taps---when a buyer first tapped, how long until they re-tapped, whether they re-tapped after seeing a property listed somewhere else---tells you something a CRM never could. It's the difference between knowing a buyer *contacted you* and knowing a buyer *kept thinking about you for two weeks*.

That's a behaviour signal QR can't give you, because nobody re-scans the same QR code twice. The physical object on their desk is what enables the re-tap.
