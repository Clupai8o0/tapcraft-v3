## What "recycled PLA" actually means

There's a common misunderstanding worth clearing up: recycled PLA isn't the same thing as compostable PLA.

PLA (polylactic acid) is *biobased*---it's made from corn starch or sugar cane, not petroleum. That's the "PL" in the name.

It's *industrially compostable* if you put it in a 60°C+ compost facility for about 90 days. Most home compost piles don't get hot enough, so PLA in your backyard compost will sit there indefinitely.

It's also *mechanically recyclable*---you can grind it up, melt it back into pellets, and re-extrude it into new filament. That's what Phoenix Polymers does.

The recycled PLA we use is option 3: it's PLA that was already printed once, ground up, re-pelletised, and re-extruded into new filament. The carbon footprint of a recycled-PLA print is about 38% lower than a virgin-PLA print of the same product, because you've skipped the corn-sugar-monomer step entirely.

It's also not *contaminated* with petroleum-derived recycled plastic from kerbside bins. The feedstock comes from clean industrial sources (our reject prints, university lab waste) where the polymer composition is known.

## How Phoenix Polymers processes it

We toured their Geelong site in March 2024 before we signed the supply agreement. Here's what their workflow looks like:

1. **Intake.** Suppliers (us, four other studios, three universities) ship boxes of reject prints. We weigh and label each box at our end before it leaves Brunswick.
2. **Sorting.** Each batch gets sorted by colour. They run separate streams for natural / white, black, and "mixed" (anything else).
3. **Grinding.** Industrial granulator reduces prints to ~4mm chips.
4. **Drying.** PLA is hygroscopic---it absorbs water from the air, which causes printing problems if not removed. Phoenix runs a 110°C drying cycle for 6 hours.
5. **Re-pelletising.** The chips go through an extruder with a melt-mixer and are reformed into 3mm round pellets.
6. **Filament extrusion.** Pellets go through a precision filament extruder that produces 1.75mm filament with ±0.03mm diameter tolerance.
7. **QC.** Diameter sampled per metre, density tested per batch, tensile tested every 10kg.

The site produces about 30 tonnes per year. We're about 15% of their output.

## What it costs

We paid AUD $14.20/kg in 2025 for the natural (slight grey-green tint) variant. The black variant is $14.85/kg---they add a small amount of carbon black pigment.

For comparison:

- Virgin PLA from a Melbourne-based reseller: $11.40/kg
- Virgin PLA from a US/EU source via Bambu Lab: $9.60/kg
- Cheap virgin PLA from Shenzhen on AliExpress: $5.80/kg (but quality varies wildly---we wouldn't use it for production)

The recycled premium is real but not catastrophic. For a typical 1,000-unit keychain run, switching from virgin to recycled PLA adds about $42 to the material bill---which is ~$0.04/unit. We round it up to +8% per unit on the customer quote, which gives us some operational margin against material yield loss (recycled PLA has slightly higher print-defect rates---about 4% vs 1.5% for virgin).

## Why customers ask for it

Three reasons our customers specify recycled PLA, in order of frequency:

1. **Corporate sustainability lines.** Big procurement teams have ESG mandates that explicitly require recycled content in branded merchandise. A coffee chain we work with has a "30% recycled content by 2027" target across all merch. Recycled PLA hits that line item.
2. **Brand storytelling.** Some customers (especially in events and hospitality) want the recycled-content narrative for press. They put a small badge on the product or packaging---"made from recycled Australian print waste"---and it lands well.
3. **Personal values.** Independent agents, single-venue owners, and small businesses choose it because they personally care. No RFP forcing them. They just want to know their merch isn't virgin plastic.

We don't pretend recycled PLA is *better* than virgin in every way. The recycled material has slightly more variance in colour (you'll see subtle banding between print batches), slightly higher defect rates, and slightly less impact strength. For 90% of branded merch products these don't matter. For high-precision parts they would.

## What we'd improve in 2026

Three things we're actively working on with Phoenix:

1. **Closing the loop on our own waste.** Currently we ship our reject prints to Phoenix, who turns them into filament we then re-purchase. We're working on a model where our specific waste comes back as our specific feedstock---which lets us tell customers exactly which products their tags came from. (Marketing benefit, not a real circular-economy improvement.)
2. **Adding PHA to the same supply chain.** PHA is industrially compostable in 90 days and we use it for events that have a one-night-only lifespan. The supply chain is currently imported from Italy. We're talking to Phoenix about whether they can process PHA locally.
3. **Per-batch carbon-footprint reporting.** Each batch of recycled PLA we use generates a different carbon-saving number depending on the inputs. We want to put that on each customer's quote PDF so they can include real numbers in their own sustainability reports.
